# Phase 6B Batch C3: Grow Content Quality Report

Grow content quality, density, and investment education. Five courses:
Are You Ready to Invest?, Stocks & Bonds Without the Hype, Deposits & Pooled
Funds, PH Government Securities, Crypto Without the Hype. Presentation and
teaching quality only. No path, course, lesson, or progress id changed; no
interaction system redesign (that is Batch D); no C4 Business, C5, or Phase 7
work started.

The headline finding shaped everything: measurement proved these courses are
NOT prose-bloated. They are interaction-heavy and already disciplined. So C3
is a precision and clarity pass, not a shortening one, exactly as the
acceptance standard asks. Word count went slightly UP in three courses,
because the highest-value changes were small decision-support additions, not
cuts.

## 1. Grow Before vs After

Before: five well-built, unusually careful courses. Every reviewer on the
panel (Philippine finance, investment education, securities, government
securities, crypto risk, behavioral finance, learning design, content
governance) independently reported the same thing: rigorously product-neutral,
no guaranteed-outcome language, decision-oriented, math correct, examples
fictional. There was no density emergency and no accuracy scandal to fix.

After: the same courses, made more precise about risk, plainer in a handful of
jargon-first spots, more neutral in the few places crypto copy leaned toward a
purchase, and more decision-honest, most importantly by finally naming why a
person would ever accept investment uncertainty (the cost of leaving long-term
money in cash) and by naming three behavioral traps (anchoring, panic selling,
FOMO) that the courses raised emotionally but never resolved.

## 4. Investing Readiness: how the curriculum answers "Am I ready?"

The readiness course supports the decision "Am I ready to invest yet?", never
"which investment should I buy?". It walks emergency buffer, high-cost debt,
cash needs, goals, time horizon, and ability to tolerate loss, and Lesson 5's
Readiness Card is explicitly "a mirror, not a gate, not a green light," never
labelling anyone ready/approved/qualified. C3 strengthened three things:
- The foundation-first principle is preserved and its two affirmative
  "guaranteed" words became "certain," so the course carries no
  guaranteed-outcome language even about debt.
- The time-horizon bands are now labelled rough rules of thumb, not official
  definitions (the authors' own code comment already admitted they could not
  be confirmed as PSE/SEC language; that honesty now reaches the learner).
- The course now answers the readiness question in BOTH directions: it added a
  neutral nugget that leaving long-term money in cash is not automatically safe
  (prices rise, buying power erodes), explicitly framed as "not a push to
  invest, only the honest trade to weigh." The course still never implies that
  finishing it means the user should invest.

## 5. Stocks & Bonds: major teaching changes

- Bond accuracy: "held to maturity a bond returns its principal" was imprecise
  for a bond bought above or below face value. It now says the issuer repays
  the bond's FACE VALUE at maturity, which equals what you lent only if you
  bought it at issue; a bond bought later for more or less than face value can
  return an amount different from what you paid.
- Behavioral: added anchoring-on-purchase-price and panic-selling nuggets to
  the returns-and-losses lesson, which already raised the "see red, stomach
  drops" feeling but never told the reader what to do with it.
- House rule: removed the one "barkada" used inside a sentence (now "group
  chat"), the only Filipino-noun-in-a-sentence violation across all five files.

## 6. Deposits & Pooled Funds: major teaching changes

- NAV accuracy: NAV was defined as "the total value of everything a fund
  holds." It now says "minus what it owes, including accrued fees," which also
  reconciles with the same course teaching that the management fee is built
  into NAVPU.
- Precision: softened the fact-sheet answer that treated a one-word "aggressive"
  risk band as fully answering "the fund's main risks"; it now says the band
  signals the risk level while the specific named risks still need reading.
- Plain language: glossed "sales load" as "a one-time charge when you buy in."
- PDIC: the ₱1,000,000 coverage figure (per depositor, per bank, effective
  2025-03-15) was independently verified as current and is held as versioned,
  dated data, so no change was needed. See register below.

## 7. Government Securities: technical complexity without implying excess risk

Kept intact and classified KEEP across all six lessons. The course teaches the
lend-to-the-government mental model before any acronym, corrects the "a
government bond cannot lose money" belief head-on, and names interest-rate/price
risk on early sale, inflation, liquidity, reinvestment, and sovereign-credit
risk explicitly. It never calls government securities risk-free and never
embeds a current rate, yield, minimum placement, or the 20 percent final tax,
directing the reader to the Bureau of the Treasury's current notice instead.
That is the correct handling of a volatile domain and was left as a strength.
Two dense prose blocks are recorded for Batch D progressive-disclosure, not
trimmed here.

## 8. Crypto: optionality, risk, and neutrality

The policy review CLEARED the course on the education-versus-advice line:
neutral both ways (not promotional, not anti-crypto), no named token or
exchange, no expected returns, no FOMO, no normalized allocation, and it
prioritizes what crypto is, why prices move sharply, custody/key risk, scams,
counterparty/platform risk, regulatory uncertainty, and irreversible mistakes.
C3 tightened the three places the copy leaned slightly toward action:
- The decision-lab action label no longer presupposes "a crypto offer" to act
  on; it now opens Money Mindset "to think a decision through before acting."
- The decision-lab opening no longer says "the pull to just try a small
  amount"; it now names the urge without the minimizing "just."
- The leverage sentence now states the loss first ("magnify a loss just as
  easily as a gain").
- Plain language: the opening definition leads with the everyday idea (a
  holding tracked on a shared digital record no single company runs) before
  the term "distributed-ledger."
- Behavioral: added a FOMO nugget beside the existing recency-bias one.

## 9. Risk-Language Audit (material wording corrections)

| Course / lesson | Before | After | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Readiness L2 | "a guaranteed reduction in what you owe" | "a certain reduction" | remove affirmative guarantee language |
| Readiness L2 | "guarantees you stop paying that interest" | "means you certainly stop paying" | same |
| Readiness L3 | time bands stated as definition | "rough rules of thumb, not official definitions" | avoid rigid unsourced formula |
| Stocks L5 | "held to maturity returns its principal" | repays "face value"; differs from price paid if bought at a premium/discount | volatility/return precision |
| Deposits L3 | NAV = "total value of everything a fund holds" | "minus what it owes, including accrued fees" | correct definition |
| Deposits L5 | risk band "answers" the fund's main risks | band signals level; specific risks still need reading | a label is not the risk list |
| Crypto L4 | "magnify a gain, and just as easily a loss" | loss stated first | do not lead with upside on leverage |

No absolute "safe" / "risk-free" claim was found to need correcting; the
courses already avoid them. Volatility, permanent-loss risk, liquidity risk,
credit/default risk, and inflation risk are distinguished where relevant
without turning beginner lessons into textbooks.
